The Man Behind the Charts

Who Was Henry Bayfield?

Henry Wolsey Bayfield arrived in the Gulf of St. Lawrence in 1827 with a small crew, a schooner called the Gulnare, and a mandate from the British Admiralty to chart one of the most treacherous and poorly mapped stretches of water in the North Atlantic world. He would not finish until 1856.

Bayfield was already a veteran hydrographer. In his twenties he had surveyed Lake Ontario, Lake Erie, and Lake Huron under the direction of William Owen — producing charts that would guide Great Lakes navigation for generations. But the Gulf of St. Lawrence was a different challenge: vast, fog-prone, studded with shoals, and swept by tidal currents that shifted from season to season. It demanded systematic, painstaking work on a scale that few officers had attempted.

"The Gulf of St. Lawrence, with its intricate channels, hidden rocks, and fogs that can last for days, presented difficulties perhaps unequalled in any similar extent of water."

— Bayfield, reflecting on the survey in his later correspondence

Working from June to October each year — ice and winter storms made year-round operations impossible — Bayfield and his crew sailed systematically from headland to headland, island to island, taking soundings, fixing positions by astronomical observation, and recording weather and sea conditions in meticulous daily journal entries. The charts he produced were so accurate that the Royal Navy and the Canadian Hydrographic Service continued to rely on them well into the twentieth century.

Bayfield eventually settled in Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island, where he died in 1885 at the age of ninety. He is buried at St. Peter's Anglican Cathedral, and his name lives on in Bayfield Inlet, Bayfield Sound, and a scattering of other features across the Gulf he spent his life mapping.

The Research Project

From Journal Pages to an Animated Map

Henry Bayfield kept detailed journals throughout the survey — recording not just hydrographic measurements but weather, ice conditions, anchorages, the names and characters of local pilots, and the slow accumulation of knowledge about a coastline that few Europeans had ever described systematically. Those journals, edited and published by historian Ian McKenzie, are a rich primary source for the environmental and maritime history of the Gulf.

Working at the UPEI GeoREACH Lab, we set out to ask a simple but difficult question: where was Bayfield on any given day? The journals record place names, distances, and directions of travel — but translating that into geographic coordinates required careful reading, cross-referencing against Bayfield's own charts, and judgment calls where the record is ambiguous.

From roughly 2,500 dated journal entries we extracted positions for each active-season day (June through October) across the full survey. We identified over 700 distinct places Bayfield named or visited, geo-referenced them against modern coordinates, and — with the help of students in the GeoREACH Lab — built a water-routing algorithm to trace plausible sailing paths between each successive position.

The result is an animated map that lets you watch the St. Lawrence Survey unfold, year by year and day by day, across more than two decades.

What You'll Discover

Trends Visible in the Data

Even a single pass through the animation reveals patterns that are hard to see from reading the journals alone. A few things to watch for:

The northward push of the early 1830s. In the first years of the survey, Bayfield concentrated on the northern shore of the Gulf — the Quebec North Shore, the Strait of Belle Isle, and the coasts of Anticosti Island. You can trace his systematic working of the coastline, anchorage by anchorage.

The pivot to the Southern Gulf. By the 1840s, the centre of gravity shifts. Bayfield's home base moves closer to Prince Edward Island, and his track lines increasingly cover the Northumberland Strait, Cape Breton, and the approaches to the Bras d'Or Lakes. The shift reflects both the progress of the survey and his deepening familiarity with the region.

The gaps in the record. Several seasons are missing — journals from the late 1830s have not survived, and the period 1848–1850 is absent from the documentary record. These gaps appear as breaks in the animation, a reminder that what we have is a partial archive, not an omniscient record.

The rhythm of the active season. June departures from Charlottetown or Quebec; a push to the farthest reaches of the survey area through July and August; a gradual retreat south as October ice threatens. The seasonal pattern repeats year after year, with individual variation depending on weather and the state of the work.

Beyond the Charts

Bayfield and the Lighthouses of the Gulf

Charting the Gulf was only part of Bayfield's contribution to maritime safety. Because he knew the coastline more intimately than anyone alive, colonial governments, the Admiralty, and the Trinity Houses of Quebec and Montreal turned to him repeatedly for advice on where to build lighthouses. At least twelve advisory engagements appear directly in his journals — and additional lighthouses across Quebec and Atlantic Canada can be traced to his surveys and recommendations through external sources.

The most intensive engagement was Cape Pine, Newfoundland, where the Admiralty specifically ordered Bayfield in 1847 to determine the site. He sailed to Trepassey Harbour, organized survey expeditions to both Cape Pine and Cape Race, personally chose the landing place and site, coordinated with Royal Engineers officers, and submitted a full report with chart to the Hydrographer and the Governor of Newfoundland. The Cape Pine lighthouse went into service in January 1851; Cape Race followed in 1855–56.

At Point Prim, PEI, Bayfield's technical specifications proved decisive. When asked by Lieutenant Governor FitzRoy for his opinion in 1841, he replied that the light "must be of large size, with a Light of sufficient power to be clearly seen at the distance of 4 or 5 leagues from the deck of a Vessel" — pushing the budget far above the initial estimate and delaying construction until 1845, when Point Prim became the first lighthouse on Prince Edward Island.

Floating Light, The Traverse (Traverse de Saint-Roch), QC
47.3660°N, 70.2384°W — Shoals of St. Roch, north of Île aux Grues
Site selected		 1830–31 In February 1830 Bayfield wrote to Trinity House of Quebec "in answer to their Letter requesting my opinion as to the most eligible place for anchoring the Light Vessel now building." The vessel was stationed at the Traverse de Saint-Roch — the treacherous shoal passage north of Crane Island (Île aux Grues) and the two small islands called The Pillars (Les Piliers). Bayfield had surveyed this precise channel in June 1830, directing Lt. Collins to "run certain lines of soundings between the Traverse & the Pillars, which will complete the South channel above the Traverse." The light vessel's necessity was underlined in June 1831 when the Gulnare herself ran aground near the Stone Pillar (Le Pilier de Fer) while Bayfield was working in the area. By September 1831 the floating light was operational: his journal records "Running for the floating Light at the Traverse under all sail" on the night of 15 Sept. 1831, and he passed it again on 19 Oct. 1832. The 1837 Bayfield chart of Bic Island to Quebec shows floating lights at the "Shoals of St. Roque" in two positions at this location.

Contrary to advice		 1862 Bayfield is on record as having advised against a lighthouse at Île de Bellechasse. The island lies in the heart of the region Bayfield charted most intensively, between Île aux Grues and the south shore approach to Quebec City.
